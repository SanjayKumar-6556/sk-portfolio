## In one line

I built two neural emulators of a cosmological signal — one deterministic, one Bayesian — and
put them inside an MCMC pipeline that infers the astrophysics of the early Universe from a
simulated radio-telescope measurement. The Bayesian one carries its own prediction uncertainty
all the way into the final parameter contours.

**Skills demonstrated:** surrogate modelling, Bayesian deep learning, calibrated uncertainty,
likelihood design, MCMC, evaluation under shrinking training data, research code packaging.

## Context

During the Epoch of Reionization, ultraviolet light from the first galaxies ionised the neutral
hydrogen filling the early Universe. The redshifted 21-cm line of that hydrogen is the most
direct probe of the era, and its spatial fluctuations are summarised by the 21-cm power
spectrum.

Predicting that power spectrum from a given set of astrophysical parameters requires a
semi-numerical simulation — expensive enough that you cannot afford to run it inside a sampler
that needs millions of forward evaluations. An **emulator** replaces the simulation with a
neural network that answers in milliseconds.

This was my MSc thesis project at the Department of Astronomy, Astrophysics & Space Engineering,
IIT Indore, supervised by Dr. Suman Majumdar, carried out between June 2023 and May 2024. The
work later became part of a peer-reviewed paper (details under *Artifacts*).

## My role

I did the emulator and inference work described on this page as my own thesis project: the
training-library preparation, both emulator families, the likelihood, the sampler, and the
analysis. On the resulting journal paper I am **second author of nine**; the 21-cm
power-spectrum emulator is the part of that paper I presented as **first author** on a
conference poster. The paper's bispectrum emulator is collaborators' work and I do not claim it.

## The problem

An existing ANN emulator (Tiwari et al.) already did the fast-prediction job, but it has a
structural limitation: it returns a single number per `k`-bin. There is no statement of how
much to trust that number. When such an emulator is used as the forward model inside Bayesian
inference, its own error cannot be propagated into the recovered parameters — so the final
error bars silently understate the truth.

Three things had to happen:

1. Make the deterministic emulator better where it was actually failing.
2. Replace the point estimate with a distribution.
3. Make the inference likelihood consume that distribution honestly.

## Approach

  caption="The loop the whole project is built around. />

**Training library.** The emulator maps three reionization parameters — minimum host-halo mass
`M_h,min` (10–800, in units of 10^8 solar masses), ionizing efficiency `N_ion` (10–200), and
photon mean free path `R_mfp` (1.12–40.32 Mpc) — onto the power spectrum at seven `k`-bins at
redshift `z ≈ 7`, from simulation boxes of side ≈ 215 cMpc. Initial training used 7,150
parameter sets.

**Improving the ANN first.** Before adding Bayesian machinery I widened the training library's
coverage of parameter space, then located the regions where the emulator's predictions were
most wrong and increased the density of training samples specifically there. That is a data
problem, not a model problem, and solving it first meant the later comparison was fair.

**The Bayesian emulator.** The BNN is a Pyro/PyTorch model, `3 → 50 → 100 → 50 → 7` with ELU
activations, with Normal priors placed over every weight and bias and a Gamma prior on the
response noise. The weight posterior is sampled with **NUTS/HMC** rather than fitted with
variational inference, so the predictive distribution comes from actual posterior samples over
the network's weights.

**The likelihood is where it pays off.** The covariance in the log-likelihood is built from
sample variance plus thermal noise for a 1000-hour SKA-Low mock observation — and, for the BNN,
plus the emulator's own predictive variance in each `k`-bin. The uncertainty is not decoration
on a plot; it is a term in the inference.

**The sampler.** A hand-written Metropolis–Hastings sampler walks the three-parameter space
inside a hard prior box, four chains launched in parallel from randomised starting points, with
posteriors summarised as corner plots.

## Trade-offs

- **NUTS over variational inference.** Variational BNNs are far cheaper to train, and that is
  what most applied work reaches for. I sampled the weight posterior instead, because the whole
  point of the exercise was whether the emulator's uncertainty is trustworthy enough to enter a
  likelihood — and a variational approximation would have made the uncertainty itself an
  approximation of unknown quality. The price is training cost.
- **A hand-written sampler over an off-the-shelf one.** Writing Metropolis–Hastings myself was
  slower than importing a library, but it kept the likelihood, the prior box, and the emulator
  call in one readable path. For a study whose conclusion is about how uncertainty flows through
  the pipeline, an opaque sampler would have been the wrong dependency.
- **Two emulator families, two frameworks.** Keeping the ANN in Keras and the BNN in Pyro meant
  maintaining two stacks. The alternative — reimplementing the ANN in PyTorch — would have made
  the comparison to the earlier published emulator less direct.

## How it was evaluated

The interesting test was not "does it fit the training set". It was **what happens as the
training data runs out**, because in practice you never have enough simulations.

I retrained both emulators at 7,150, 1,000, 500 and 200 training samples and ran the full
inference at each size, for three different fiducial reionization scenarios. Evaluation looked
at per-`k`-bin prediction against held-out spectra, and at the recovered posterior contours —
whether the true parameters sit inside the credible region, and how the contours change as the
emulator degrades.

The finding: with abundant data both emulators behave similarly, but as the training set
shrinks the ANN becomes confidently wrong — biased predictions with no signal that anything is
off — while the BNN's predictions stay consistent with truth inside a widening uncertainty band.
An emulator that widens its error bars when it is under-trained is usable; one that does not is
a trap.

I also diagnosed the weakest part of the result rather than hiding it: `R_mfp` is the least
well-constrained of the three parameters, and I produced separate diagnostics showing why the
power spectrum is relatively insensitive to it over much of the range.

- Two working emulators, ANN and BNN, for the 21-cm power spectrum at `z ≈ 7`.
- An inference pipeline in which emulator uncertainty, sample variance and instrument noise are
  combined in a single covariance.
- A robustness study across four training-set sizes and three fiducial scenarios showing the BNN
  degrades gracefully where the ANN does not.
- The work contributed to a peer-reviewed journal paper (below), whose broader conclusion is
  that BNN emulators capture prediction uncertainty for both the power spectrum and the
  bispectrum and give tighter constraints when used inside the inference pipeline.

## What I would do differently

- **The train/infer handoff was never a contract.** Training writes checkpoints under a
  timestamped path; inference looks for a fixed one. I would define the artefact path in one
  place and have both sides import it, rather than agreeing by convention.
- **No entrypoints.** The training and inference modules execute their pipeline at import time.
  It works when you run them as modules and is a hazard the moment anything imports them. A
  `main()` behind a guard costs nothing and I skipped it.
- **Shipped defaults were smoke-test defaults.** The committed configuration carries tiny sample
  counts used for quick checks, not the values that produce the published results. Anyone
  cloning the repo runs a toy by accident. Config presets for "smoke" and "paper" would have
  fixed this.
- **I would export calibration plots on every run**, not at the end. Coverage — how often the
  truth lands inside the stated interval — is the number that decides whether an uncertainty
  estimate is worth anything, and it deserved to be a standing artefact rather than an analysis
  step.
