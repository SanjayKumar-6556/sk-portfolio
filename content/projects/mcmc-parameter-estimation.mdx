## In one line

Type Ia supernovae are standard candles: their distance modulus traces how the Universe has
expanded. This project recovers the posterior on two cosmological parameters from that data with
a sampler written from scratch, and then tries three separate ways to prove the sampler is
telling the truth.

**Skills demonstrated:** implementing MCMC from first principles, likelihood construction with a
full covariance matrix, convergence diagnostics, cross-checking a result by an independent
method.

## Why it is worth reading

This is a small project and it is on the site for one reason: it is the foundation under the
[Bayesian emulator work](/projects/bayesian-neural-network). Anyone can import a sampler. Being
able to write the sampler, choose the proposal, and then argue about whether the chains have
actually converged is what makes the emulator project's claims about uncertainty something I can
defend rather than repeat.

## What it does

The model predicts the distance modulus `mu(z)` as a function of the dimensionless Hubble
constant `h` and the matter density parameter `Omega_m`. A Gaussian log-likelihood compares that
prediction to observed distance moduli, and a random-walk Metropolis–Hastings sampler explores
the two-parameter space.

The likelihood uses the **full 31×31 measurement covariance matrix**, inverted once and applied
as a quadratic form — not the diagonal approximation. Supernova measurements are correlated;
throwing those correlations away makes the posterior look tighter than the data supports, which
is precisely the failure mode this kind of analysis exists to avoid.

## Convergence, three ways

A single chain that looks converged proves nothing, so the project checks it from three
directions:

1. **Multiple chains from deliberately different starting points**, to show they land on the
   same posterior rather than on wherever they started.
2. **The Gelman–Rubin statistic**, comparing between-chain and within-chain variance across
   those chains.
3. **Importance sampling** as an independent estimator of the same posterior — a different
   algorithm agreeing is stronger evidence than the same algorithm agreeing with itself.

The acceptance ratio is analysed rather than assumed, and the notebooks include an animated
demonstration of a chain converging, which is the clearest way I know to show a non-specialist
what a sampler is actually doing.

## An extension worth the detour

A separate notebook tackles the **Eddington problem**: fitting a straight line when *both*
variables carry measurement uncertainty. Ordinary least squares quietly assumes the independent
variable is exact; when it is not, the fitted slope is biased. Handling it properly means writing
a likelihood that accounts for uncertainty in both directions — a small problem that is much less
straightforward than it first appears, and a good sanity check on whether you actually understand
the model you are fitting.

## What I would do differently

- **Report effective sample size**, not just acceptance ratio and Gelman–Rubin. It is the number
  that tells you how much independent information a chain of a given length really contains.
- **Tune the proposal deliberately.** The step sizes were chosen by hand; an adaptive proposal
  or a documented tuning pass would make the sampler's efficiency a measured property rather
  than a setting.
