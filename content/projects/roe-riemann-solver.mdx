## In one line

No library does the physics here. The numerics — the Riemann solve, the flux limiter, the
curvilinear geometry, the positivity fallback — are written out by hand, validated against a
benchmark with a known answer, and then pointed at a problem where the answer is not known in
advance.

**Skills demonstrated:** numerical methods from first principles, stability and positivity
handling, validating a solver before trusting it, translating published physics into working
code.

## Context

MSc computational astrophysics coursework at IIT Indore. I am labelling it as coursework

here because it is the clearest evidence I have that I can implement numerics rather than call
them.

## The problem

The Euler equations describe an inviscid compressible gas. In one dimension they are a
hyperbolic system of conservation laws, and they have an inconvenient property: even perfectly
smooth initial conditions develop discontinuities — shocks and contact surfaces. So they are
solved in finite-volume form, updating cell averages from fluxes evaluated at cell interfaces,
and at every interface you face a Riemann problem: two constant states separated by a jump.

Solving that exactly at every interface, every timestep, is too expensive. That is what
approximate Riemann solvers exist for.

## Approach

**Roe's solver.** The system is linearised about a specially constructed average state, and the
jump at the interface is decomposed onto the eigenvectors of the flux Jacobian — the two
acoustic families travelling at `u ± c` and the entropy wave travelling at `u`. Each wave is
then upwinded according to its own direction of travel.

**Superbee limiter.** First-order upwinding is stable but smears every feature you care about.
A flux limiter blends toward a second-order flux where the solution is smooth and drops back to
first order near discontinuities, which keeps shocks sharp without introducing oscillations
around them.

**Validation before application.** The solver is exercised on Sod-type shock-tube problems,
where a diaphragm separates high- and low-pressure gas and the exact solution is known. That
test hits all three wave families — rarefaction, contact, shock — at once, which is exactly why
it is the standard benchmark and exactly why it is worth running before believing anything else
the code says.

**The actual application.** Fast supernova ejecta ploughing into the slower wind the star shed
before exploding, in spherical coordinates, following the self-similar picture of Chevalier
(1982). A forward shock runs into the wind, a reverse shock runs back into the ejecta, and a
contact discontinuity sits between them. Because the shocked gas is dense, radiative cooling
becomes dynamically important, and it is included as a source term using the tabulated cooling
curve of Dalgarno & McCray (1972). With cooling switched on, the shocked shell collapses into a
thin dense layer — a qualitatively different regime from the adiabatic case.

## The part I am most pleased with

The spherical solver carries volume-weighted state vectors and geometric source terms, and it
has a **positivity limiter**: if a proposed update would drive density or pressure negative, the
scheme falls back to first-order fluxes for that step rather than producing a number that is
arithmetically fine and physically impossible.

Knowing that a high-order scheme can step outside the physically admissible state — and building
the fallback before it bites — is the difference between a solver that runs and a solver you can
leave running. It is the same instinct as writing the invalid-state guard in a data pipeline,
and it is why this project is on a portfolio that is otherwise about AI systems.

## What I would do differently

- **The validation notebook ships without its outputs.** The one notebook that demonstrates the
  solver is correct renders as empty cells. Re-running and committing it costs minutes and it is
  the first thing a reviewer would want to see.
- **No convergence study.** Claiming second-order accuracy in smooth regions is worth a
  measurement — refine the grid, plot the error, show the slope — and I asserted it from the
  scheme rather than demonstrating it from the code.
